Most advice about AI and human judgement stays vague. One practitioner has written the boundary down, case by case, and the list copies into other kinds of work almost unchanged.

The source has no figures at all. That is a limit, and it is also why the content is useful.

## Where the list comes from

TNGlobal published a Q&A on 3 September 2026 with Yang Li, a Partner in Immigration at Vialto, interviewed by J. Angelo Racoma. Vialto sells immigration and global mobility services, so Li is describing a service his firm sells. He has a commercial interest in the conclusion that human expertise stays necessary.

Immigration casework is a good test bed for this reader. It is paperwork-heavy, rule-bound and professional, and the cost of a mistake falls on a named person. Most managers run something that looks similar.

## What he gives the tool

Li hands the tool structured work. It identifies possible visa pathways, checks basic eligibility criteria and finds missing documentation. It also handles the administration around a case.

Each of those has a clear right answer or a clear gap. A document is present or it is not. A basic criterion is met or it is not.

## What he keeps for a person

This is the part to copy. Li lists the situations that go to a person.

- Cases involving regulatory ambiguity
- Complex family circumstances
- Prior immigration refusals
- Criminal history
- Security concerns
- Any situation where government discretion materially changes the outcome

Look at what the list has in common. In each case the answer depends on judgement, discretion or a person's circumstances, and a wrong answer is expensive. The tool is allowed near the paperwork and kept away from the decision.

## What he measures

Li does not measure speed. He names five measures instead.

**Assessment accuracy.** Whether the first view was right.

**Reduction in rework.** Whether work stops coming back for correction.

**Consistency of recommendations.** Whether similar cases get similar answers.

**Compliance outcomes.** Whether the result stands up to a regulator.

**Successful application rates.** Whether the thing you were trying to achieve actually happened.

Speed is missing from the list on purpose. A tool that makes work faster and less accurate has not helped, and speed alone would never show that.

## Why one approach does not cross borders

Li also says one model does not cover the region. Governments across Asia-Pacific differ in their immigration frameworks, labour market priorities and documentation standards. A single standard approach does not transfer.

The wider point applies at home as well. If your rules differ by market, client or regulator, a tool that works well in one place may need a different list of cases somewhere else.

## How to draw your own list

Start from mistakes, not from the tool. Think of the last three errors in your team that cost real time or money. For each, ask what made that case hard.

Group the answers. You will usually find a handful of triggers, such as unclear rules, an unusual customer situation, or a decision where somebody has discretion. Those triggers become your list.

Then write the list where the team can see it. A list nobody can find will not be followed. Review it after a month, because the first version will be wrong in both directions.

## What to do this month

**Write your own list of cases that go to a person.** Aim for five or six items, in plain language. Base it on the last few mistakes, not on guesswork.

**Share it with the people who do the work.** Ask what they would add. They know which cases feel risky before anything goes wrong.

**Pick three measures that are not speed.** Use Li's list as a prompt. Accuracy, rework and consistency are available in almost any team.

**Review it at the end of the month.** Check which cases went to a person and which should have. Adjust the list.

## What the interview leaves out

There are no figures anywhere in the piece. It gives no sample, no percentages and no results. It describes how one practitioner operates, and it cannot show how well that works.

It is also one firm's view, from a company that sells the service. Li may be right, and the list is still a starting point to test against your own cases.

## A worked example outside immigration

Take a finance team that checks supplier invoices. A tool can match each invoice to its purchase order, flag a missing approval and spot a duplicate. Those are the structured checks, and they have clear answers.

The list for a person might read like this. An invoice that disagrees with the order by more than a set amount. A new supplier with no history. A payment request marked urgent by a senior colleague. A dispute that involves a contract term.

Each of those depends on judgement or on a relationship, and a wrong call costs money or trust. The measures follow the same logic. Count wrong matches, count invoices sent back for correction, and check whether two similar invoices get the same treatment. Speed is a useful extra, and it is never the test.

## The takeaway

Write down which cases must go to a person before the tool arrives. Measure the work by accuracy, rework and consistency, not by speed alone. A boundary written in advance is easier to defend than one drawn after something goes wrong.
