Attackers broke into one software company and, through it, reached the data of roughly 200 of that
company's customers.

None of those 200 organisations were breached. None of them made a mistake. None of them had any
say in it, and most would have had no way to know it had happened.

That figure comes from Anthropic's threat intelligence report, published on 10 September, covering
operations it disrupted between December 2025 and August 2026.

## Why a security report is a procurement story

Most of that report is not for you. It covers surveillance, influence operations, biological misuse
and illicit distillation, across more than 50 targeted organisations in education, retail, energy,
technology, healthcare, finance and government. If your department is not running a security
programme, there is nothing in those pages you can act on.

One finding is different, and it is the supply chain one.

Attackers are now deliberately compromising software suppliers as a route to the customers behind
them. Not as an accident of scale. As the method.

That changes what the reader is exposed to. Your own systems can be in good order, your own staff
can be careful, your own controls can be exactly what your insurer asked for, and your data can
still be sitting inside a supplier who is having a bad month. You cannot audit your way out of that
from inside your own building.

## Three hours, start to finish

Two numbers in the report are worth carrying, because they settle a question people usually get
wrong.

One enterprise software compromise went from a single stolen developer token to full administrative
control in roughly three hours. Another reached over 2,100 Azure AD token sets, across more than 40
corporate tenants, in about 34 hours.

The instinct is to picture a breach as a slow thing. Someone gets in, moves quietly, and weeks later
the damage is understood. That picture is where "we would notice" comes from.

Three hours is shorter than a morning. It is shorter than the gap between a supplier noticing
something odd and a supplier telling anybody. Whatever your response plan is, it is not going to
start inside three hours, and it does not need to. What matters is that the timeline has moved and
your assumptions probably have not.

## The question that belongs in a conversation you already have

You already talk to your suppliers. There is a renewal, or a review, or an account manager who wants
half an hour. That conversation has a shape and you know how to have it.

Three questions fit inside it without needing anyone technical in the room.

What AI systems do you run that touch our data? Not what AI features do you offer us. What is
running on your side, on our information.

Who audits that, and when did they last do it? An answer naming an external party and a date is
worth something. An answer describing a commitment is worth nothing.

If something goes wrong on your side, what will you tell us, and how quickly? This is the one people
skip, and it is the one that produces the most revealing silence.

You are not trying to catch anybody out. You are establishing whether the supplier has thought about
it, because a supplier who has will answer in specifics and a supplier who has not will answer in
adjectives.

## What this does not mean

It does not mean stop buying software. Every organisation runs on suppliers and the alternative to
supplier risk is building everything yourself, which is worse in every direction.

It does not mean your data is currently compromised. It means the route exists and is being used
deliberately, which is a different claim and a smaller one.

And it does not mean the answer is a policy. A policy about supplier AI exposure written by someone
who has not asked any supplier the three questions above is a document about an imagined situation.
Ask first. Write afterwards, if the answers turn out to warrant it.

## Who is telling you this

Anthropic is reporting on misuse of Anthropic's own product. That is a company account of its own
investigations, with no independent verification attached, and it should be attributed that way in
any conversation where it carries weight.

It is still worth reading, and the reason is worth being precise about. A vendor publishing research
that concludes you should buy the vendor's product is selling. A vendor publishing what went wrong
inside its own systems is doing something else. The incentive runs against disclosure, which is
exactly what makes the disclosure interesting.

Compare it with the same week's alternative. A security firm published figures showing frontier
models bypassing endpoint detection 40 per cent of the time, generated on that firm's own testing
range, with no external sample and no independent instrument. Striking numbers, and they are the
firm's own marketing. The difference is not the quality of the research. It is who benefits from the
conclusion.

## The part that transfers

Your supplier list is longer than you think, and most of it was approved by somebody who no longer
works on it.

Pick the three suppliers who hold the most of your information. Not the three you spend the most
with, though there is usually overlap. The three who would cause the most difficulty if their
systems were open to somebody else for an afternoon.

Ask them the three questions. Write down what they say.

That is an hour of work and it produces something you did not have: a record of what your suppliers
claim, in their words, dated. If the answers are good, you have peace of mind that cost an hour. If
they are vague, you have found out now rather than during the incident, which is the only time these
questions get asked at speed and the worst time to be asking them for the first time.

Two hundred organisations found out the other way.
