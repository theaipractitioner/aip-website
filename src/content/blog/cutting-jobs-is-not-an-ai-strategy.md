The most common way to frame a first AI project is also the most damaging. Someone asks what it will save, and the answer that gets approved is a headcount.

The evidence so far suggests that story is thinner than the headlines. It also suggests the framing leads to worse results than starting with the work itself.

## What the HBR authors argue

In *Harvard Business Review* on 28 August 2026, Faisal Hoque, Thomas Davenport and Paul Scade argue that AI transformation means redesigning work, not cutting roles. They say many layoff announcements are better understood as AI-washing. That means ordinary restructuring packaged in AI language to reassure investors that leaders have a grip on the technology.

HBR's own summary adds that early AI-driven layoffs have often failed to deliver the expected returns. It says they exposed gaps in organisational knowledge and created new demands for human oversight, which has led some employers to reverse course. The recommended alternative starts with the work: break roles into tasks, find where AI genuinely improves performance, and redesign the process around the right mix of people and technology.

A note on what was read. The full article sits behind HBR's paywall. Everything above comes from the published summary and the opening paragraphs, so this post does not describe the rest.

## What the best number says

The authors cite one figure, and it is attributed to Goldman Sachs. Goldman Sachs Research, in an April 2026 note by the economist Elsie Peng, estimated that AI reduced monthly US payroll growth by roughly 16,000 jobs over the past year. It put the effect on the unemployment rate at about 0.1 percentage points.

Four details change how that number should be read.

- **It is a net figure.** Goldman separates jobs where AI substitutes for workers from jobs where it augments them. Where AI augments, it estimates payroll growth rose by about 9,000 jobs a month.
- **It is an inference.** Fortune noted that the framework rests on regression analysis, not a direct count of jobs lost to AI.
- **It is American.** The data covers the US labour market and may not describe yours.
- **It includes slower hiring.** The HBR authors point out that the drag covers fewer new jobs as well as outright job elimination.

The authors also say these numbers may well increase, but that for now we are much earlier in the process than the prevailing story suggests. That is a judgement about timing, and it could prove wrong.

## Why a headcount business case goes wrong

The following is our reasoning, not the HBR authors' findings. It is worth testing against your own experience.

When a first project carries a job saving, the people who know the work best have every reason to keep that knowledge to themselves. The person asked to explain how an invoice really gets approved is the person whose role is on the line. A tool that has never seen the exceptions gets built, and the exceptions turn out to be where the work was.

A headcount target also fixes the answer before the question is asked. If the case says four roles, the project is judged on four roles, even if the better result was faster turnaround or fewer errors. You may end up with a smaller organisation that works worse, which is not the same as a better one.

## What to do with a first project

Start with the work, and keep any job out of the business case.

1. **Break one role into tasks.** Pick a role your team understands well and list what it actually involves in a typical week, including the awkward parts.
2. **Mark where AI could help.** Look for tasks that are repetitive, text-heavy or slow, and note which need judgement or accountability that must stay with a person.
3. **Measure the work, not the worker.** Choose turnaround time, error rate or rework as the measure, and record today's figure first.
4. **Say what the project is not.** If no job is attached to the business case, tell the team so in plain words before the pilot starts.

The fourth step matters most. People will tell you how the work really runs only when they are not afraid of the answer.

## A short invented example

Take an accounts payable team of six, asked to find savings with AI. The headcount version of the project says the tool will handle invoice matching and so two roles can go. Everyone on the team hears that, and the senior clerk stops mentioning the supplier who always sends invoices in a different format.

The redesign version starts with the tasks. Invoice matching takes about a third of the week, queries from suppliers take another third, and the rest is exceptions that only one or two people understand. The team agrees to try the tool on matching, measure how long a batch takes and how often it needs correcting, and decide afterwards what to do with the time freed. The same tool is used, but the clerk now has a reason to explain the exceptions.

## Where this argument stops

Two limits are worth stating. The Goldman estimate describes the US economy, and an individual company can still cut roles for sound reasons that have little to do with AI. Nothing here says that no job will be affected, and the same authors expect the numbers to rise.

The narrower claim is the one to keep. A first AI project framed as a saving on headcount is usually restructuring with a new label, and the evidence for large job effects so far is smaller than the confidence of the headlines.
