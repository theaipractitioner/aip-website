Most departments start with a tool and look for somewhere to put it. A Philippine company started with a list of complaints. That costs nothing to try, and it needs no technical knowledge.

The source is one executive describing her own programme. It offers a method, not evidence that the method works.

## Where the method comes from

TNGlobal published a Q&A on 25 August 2026 with Charee Lanza, Chief People Officer at Maya, a Philippine fintech company. It is an interview, so everything in it is Lanza's account of her own organisation. Nobody outside the company has checked it.

She has every reason for her programme to read well. Keep that in mind throughout.

## What Maya did

Maya ran an AI Summit and asked staff to look at their own processes. The brief was to name work that was repetitive, slow or difficult. Lanza's one-line summary of the method is "Start with a problem people already complain about."

Two things surfaced. One was automating management reports. The other was handling employee benefits queries.

Neither is sophisticated. That is the point. The first uses came from the ordinary friction people already feel, not from a list of what the technology can do.

## Why it inverts the usual order

Most departments begin with a tool. Someone buys access, then asks the team to find uses. The list that comes back is short and vague, because people are being asked to imagine.

A complaint list works the other way round. Everyone has one. People can name the report they dread and the question they answer forty times a month. They know where the work is dull without being told what AI can do.

That makes the list cheap to produce and hard to argue with. A manager can collect it in an afternoon, and nobody needs to understand the technology first.

## The test she offers for success

Lanza gives a second idea, from the other end. She says that if employees have more technology but their work has not become meaningfully better, that is not transformation.

It is a test a non-technical manager can use. Counting how many people have logged in or how many tools you own measures adoption. Asking whether the work got better measures the thing you wanted.

Most organisations count the first. Few ask the second.

## Who gets trained

Maya also runs AI learning as part of its talent development path, across product, risk, compliance, operations, legal and People. Lanza says it is not just for engineers.

This matters because it removes the idea that AI is a technical subject. If the compliance team and the legal team are on the same path, the questions come from the work, not from the technology.

She also says AI can support a decision but should not own it. That line is short, and it matches what many managers already believe.

## What the interview leaves out

There are no numbers anywhere in the piece. It gives no hours saved, no volumes, no error rates and no headcount. Nothing in it is evidence that the approach worked. It describes what Maya did and not what happened.

The interview does not address staff fear or resistance, so do not look to it for that. It covers one company, in one country, in one function.

## How to run a complaint list

Keep it small and keep it honest. Ask each person for two things: a task they find repetitive or slow, and a task they find hard.

Collect them without judging. Sort the list by how often each task happens and how many people it affects. A weekly irritation shared by six people beats a monthly one that affects one.

Then pick one item to try. Lanza's own advice is to pick a manageable problem, learn from it and build from there. A first project that can be finished in a few weeks will teach you more than a large one that stalls.

## What to do this month

**Ask your team for their complaint list.** Two questions: what is repetitive or slow, and what is hard. Give people a week to answer.

**Sort the list by frequency and by headcount.** Look for work that many people do often. Put aside anything rare or political.

**Pick one item small enough to finish.** Write down how long it takes now, so you can compare later.

**Ask the Lanza question when you finish.** Has the work become meaningfully better? If it has not, count that as a result too.

## What a first list tends to contain

Lanza says Maya's first uses were management reports and benefits queries. Those two examples share a shape. Both recur on a calendar or in volume, both follow a pattern, and both are tedious without being risky.

Your own list will probably look similar. Expect weekly reports, repeated questions from colleagues, chasing information from other teams, and formatting documents to a standard. These are unglamorous, and that is useful. A modest first project builds confidence and gives the team a result they can see.

Watch for items that look easy and are not. A task that depends on judgement or on knowing the people involved may belong on the list for a later round. Start with the work where a mistake is cheap and obvious, and leave the delicate tasks until the team has learned how the tool behaves.

## The takeaway

Skip the question of where to put the tool. Ask your team what they already complain about, and start there. Then judge the result by whether the work got better.
