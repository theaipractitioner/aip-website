A tool your team pays for can lose its own supplier because of a dispute between two other companies. Nobody asks your opinion, and you may get about ten weeks' notice.

That is what is happening to a coding tool called Cursor this month. You may never use it, but the question it raises applies to every AI tool in your department.

## What has been reported

Cursor is a coding tool built by a company called Anysphere. Reports from CNBC, Engadget and Benzinga say SpaceX acquired it for about $60 billion, with the deal completing on 14 August 2026.

OpenAI then said it will end Cursor's access to its models on 12 November 2026. Reports say OpenAI notified SpaceX of the decision on 28 August. The stated reason is that OpenAI cannot be confident the new owner will keep to its terms of service.

OpenAI models make up about 5% of Cursor's traffic. That figure is attributed to Cursor's chief executive, Michael Truell, who has publicly pushed for a resolution. Most Cursor customers are therefore not directly affected.

We have not read OpenAI's own announcement. Its page was not readable when this was written, so the figures here come from the reporting outlets. Check the current position before you rely on any of it, because a resolution may change the date.

## Why this matters if you never use Cursor

Set the details aside and look at the shape of the story. A company sells a tool. The tool runs on a model made by a second company. The first company is bought by a third, and the second company decides it does not want to supply the third.

Customers of the tool had no part in any of it. They did not choose the acquisition and they did not choose the dispute. They simply had a tool that worked, and now part of it will not.

That structure is common. Many of the AI tools a department buys are wrappers around someone else's model. The name on the invoice is not the name of the technology underneath.

## What a change can look like

The effects of a supplier change are rarely dramatic. They are usually small and awkward.

The tool may get slower or more expensive. A feature may disappear because it depended on one model. The answers may change in tone or accuracy, and a process your team built around the old behaviour may stop working.

In this story, most customers are not affected, because the share of traffic is small. That is why it is a governance story and not an outage story. The point is that you would not know whether you were in the affected group unless you had asked beforehand.

## Why the notice period matters

The notice in this story is about ten weeks between the date OpenAI told the new owner and the date access ends. That is long enough for a large customer to plan. It is short for a department that has to ask for budget, test an alternative and retrain people.

Many small teams have no formal list of what they pay for. Subscriptions sit on personal cards, and the person who set one up may have moved on. A change that would take a week to handle with a list can take a month without one.

Ask the person who set each one up what they remember about the terms. Their answer will often be shorter than you expect.

The cost of finding out in advance is an afternoon. The cost of finding out afterwards is a stalled task and an urgent search for a replacement.

## Three questions for every tool you pay for

You can answer these in an afternoon, and the answers stay useful.

**Whose model is underneath?** Ask the supplier directly. Some will name the model, and some will say it varies. Either answer tells you something.

**What happens to us if that changes?** Does the supplier promise notice? Can you leave without a penalty? Is there a second model they could switch to?

**What would we do the next day?** If the tool stopped working or changed behaviour tomorrow, which task would be stuck, and who would notice first?

## What to do this month

**List the AI tools your team pays for.** Include the ones bought on a personal card and claimed back. Most departments find more than they expected.

**Write the supplier's name and the model's name beside each.** Where you do not know the model, mark it and ask. Keep the list to one page.

**Ask each supplier one question in writing.** "If your model supplier changes, what notice will we receive?" Keep the reply with your records.

**Pick the one tool you would miss most.** Write down what you would do if it changed overnight. Even a rough plan is better than none.

## The takeaway

The Cursor story will be settled between large companies, and your department will not be consulted about the outcome. That is the lesson.

You cannot control who sits underneath your tools. You can know who they are, and you can decide in advance what you will do if one of them leaves.
