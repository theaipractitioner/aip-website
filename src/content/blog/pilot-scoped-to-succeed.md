A pilot proves less than it appears to. It shows that a tool can work in one corner, under conditions someone chose, while nothing else changes.

That is a real result. It answers one question, which is whether the tool can do the task. It does not answer the question a manager actually faces, which is whether the tool will keep working once the rest of the organisation touches it.

## What Nambiar said

Rahul Nambiar is the chief executive and co-founder of Botsync, a Singapore company that makes software to coordinate fleets of warehouse and factory robots. In a Q&A with TNGlobal on 1 September 2026, he was asked why so many robot pilots never become working deployments. His answer was about scope, not technology.

A pilot, he said, has to show that automation can work in one corner of a facility under controlled conditions. A production deployment has to survive changing layouts, fluctuating volumes and links to existing systems, every day. The gap between the two is what he calls the integration layer. Most facilities run a patchwork of machines and software from different vendors that were never built to talk to each other.

Two cautions about the source. Botsync sells software that sits in exactly that gap, so the diagnosis is also the company's product. And this is one executive in one interview. The argument below rests on the logic of his point, not on any figure from it, and the company's growth numbers are left out on purpose.

## Strip out the robots

Nothing in the argument depends on robots. Swap the factory for a finance team, a sales desk or an HR function and it reads the same.

A pilot is scoped to succeed. Someone picks one process, one small group, a short window and a clean set of inputs. The people involved are keen, and a colleague close by fixes problems as they appear. Under those conditions a good tool will usually work.

Production asks something harder. It asks whether the tool still works when everything the pilot held still starts to move. Volumes spike at month end, a template changes, and a colleague who was never in the pilot receives the output with no idea how it was made.

## An invented example, to show the shape

A finance team pilots an AI tool to draft the commentary on its monthly variance report. One senior analyst runs it for three months. She cleans the spreadsheet first, reads every draft, and rewrites anything that sounds off.

The pilot is a success. Drafts take twenty minutes instead of two hours, and she is delighted. Then the tool goes to the whole team.

Five other analysts format their spreadsheets differently. The report feeds a board pack owned by another department, which expects a fixed layout. Nobody has time to read every draft as closely as she did. The tool has not got worse. The pilot simply never met any of this.

## What a pilot holds still

Before anyone calls a pilot a success, write down what was kept constant to make it work. Most teams find the list is longer than they expected. Typical entries look like this:

- The input was tidied by hand before the tool saw it.
- One enthusiastic person checked every result.
- The task was the same each week.
- Nobody downstream had to change how they worked.
- The tool stood alone and did not need to hand work to anything else.

Each entry is a condition that disappears at rollout. None of them is a flaw in the pilot. They are the reason it worked, and the reason the result does not transfer on its own.

## The integration layer is the rest of your organisation

Nambiar's term is technical. The manager's version is plainer. The integration layer is everything the pilot did not touch: the approval step, the spreadsheet that feeds the report, the system of record, the colleague who reformats the output, and the team that owns the data.

These connections rarely appear in a vendor demo, because demos are built in a corner. They appear in the first month of rollout, and they decide whether a good pilot becomes a good deployment.

## Why the second team is the better test

A first pilot can succeed by being fenced off. A second team cannot be fenced off in the same way, because it differs from the first in ways nobody chose.

That difference is useful. Someone has to ask what changed between the two teams, and the answer is the integration work, now visible and small enough to fix. A second department is easier than the first only if the first was connected to the rest of the business and not walled off from it.

## What to do this month

Three steps, none of which needs a technical background.

1. **List what the pilot held still.** Ask the person who ran it. They know, and they will usually say it without being pressed.
2. **Pick a second corner that differs on purpose.** Choose a different team, messier inputs or a busier week. Run the tool there before declaring success.
3. **Name an owner for every connection.** For each person or system the tool must hand work to, write down who owns it and what happens when it fails.

If the pilot cannot survive step two, you have learned that cheaply. A failed second corner in week six costs far less than a stalled rollout in month six.

## Where this argument stops

This is an argument drawn from one interview, not a study. It does not say how many pilots stall or why. It cannot tell you whether any particular tool will cope with your organisation.

What it gives you is a question to put to any pilot in any department: what was held still. The question costs nothing, and it is the one a demo will never raise.
