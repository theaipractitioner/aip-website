Close to half of office workers say they are uncomfortable telling their manager they used AI.

Not uncomfortable using it. Uncomfortable admitting it.

If you are trying to work out why nothing much is happening in your department, start there rather
than with the training budget. The blocker is not capability, and it is not access. It is that the
people you are asking to try something have quietly decided it is safer not to mention it.

## Where the number comes from

Marcus Loh, a communications professional writing in TNGlobal Insider on 21 August, cites a Slack
survey with that finding.

Worth being precise about what that is. It is an opinion column, not reporting and not research, and
the survey is cited without a link, so treat the figure as directional rather than as established
fact.

Even at its most cautious, the shape holds. A large minority of people are using these tools and
choosing not to say so, and Loh's argument is that what they are avoiding is a judgement about them
rather than a judgement about the work.

## What Loh was actually arguing

His subject is Anthropic's text watermark, which now embeds an invisible signature in Claude's
output to comply with the European AI Act's transparency requirements.

His concern is not the technology. It is that a detectable marker turns a question of quality into a
question of provenance:

> "A watermark can indicate that Claude was involved somewhere in the process, but it cannot measure
> the extent of that involvement."

That is the crux. A marker tells you a tool was present. It tells you nothing about whether the
person understood the output, checked it, disagreed with parts of it, or rewrote half of it. So the
same signal covers the person who used AI well and the person who pasted without reading, and if
the organisation has no way to tell those apart, everyone has an incentive to stay quiet.

I think Loh is right that the workplace consequence of better detection is stigma rather than
transparency. But the more useful reading for a manager is one step earlier, because the stigma is
already there and no watermark created it.

## Why this is a management problem

Consider what a department looks like where half the AI use is undeclared.

You cannot see what is working. Somebody has found a way to cut two hours off a weekly report and
nobody else will ever learn it, because sharing it means admitting to it.

You cannot fix what is not working. The person quietly pasting unchecked output into client
documents is doing the single most dangerous version of this, and they are the least likely of
anyone to raise their hand.

You cannot train people in a practice nobody admits to. Every training conversation starts by asking
people to describe what they currently do, and you will get a room of blank faces.

And you cannot manage the risk, because the risk has moved somewhere you cannot look at it. That is
worse than a team not using AI at all. A team not using it has a capability gap. A team using it in
secret has a capability gap and a governance gap, and only one of those shows up in a report.

## The standard that actually works

The fix is not better detection. It is a stated standard that makes disclosure safe, and the version
that works is about ownership of the finished work rather than disclosure of the tool.

Sophie Alpert put it about as well as it can be put in her own written policy on AI-assisted
writing:

> "You must stand behind every idea and every sentence in your docs. It is your responsibility to
> make sure that the entire document is representative of your own thoughts before you share it."

Her corollary is the useful half: it is not acceptable to reply "oh sorry, AI wrote that".

Notice what that standard does. It stops asking whether a tool was involved, which is unanswerable
and unenforceable and a bit undignified. It asks whether you are prepared to defend what left your
desk, which is exactly what you already ask of everyone about everything.

It also removes the cheating frame entirely. Nobody thinks you cheated by using a spreadsheet, a
calculator or a colleague, because in each case you remain answerable for the result. The
uncomfortable half of your team is not confused about the ethics. They are waiting to find out which
rule they are being judged against, and in the absence of a stated one, the safe assumption is the
harshest one.

## The ten-minute version

You do not need a policy document. You need to say something out loud, and then write it down
somewhere findable.

Three sentences will cover it. Using AI to help produce your work is fine and expected. You are
responsible for what you send, the same as always, whatever helped you produce it. If it goes wrong,
"the AI wrote it" is not an answer, and neither is anything else you would not have accepted before.

Then say the second part, which matters more: nobody is in trouble for anything they did before this
conversation. Without that, you have announced a standard and simultaneously created a group of
people with a reason to keep hiding.

Say it in a team meeting rather than an email, so people can see the reaction on your face while you
say it. Then go first: describe something you used it for this week, including a bit where it was
wrong and you caught it. The point of going first is not modelling good practice. It is proving that
admitting to it is survivable.

## The question

Has anyone in your organisation said out loud whether using AI is something you own or something you
hide?

Most have not. Most have a policy about data and nothing at all about disclosure, which leaves every
individual to guess. The guess they make is the cautious one, and the cost of that caution is the
thing you were blaming on the training budget.
