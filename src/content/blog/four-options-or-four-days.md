Your last strategy offsite probably ended with three or four options on the wall. The number may say more about the calendar than about your market.

That is the argument at the heart of a *Harvard Business Review* article by Felipe Csaszar. It is an argument and not a research finding, and it is worth treating as one. It also asks you to change very little, which is unusual for AI advice.

## The room you have already sat in

Csaszar chairs the strategy area at the University of Michigan's Ross School of Business. His article, in the September to October 2026 issue and online from 1 September, opens with a scene most senior people will recognise.

The team spent weeks preparing. People were flown in, a room was booked, a facilitator was hired. After two days of debate, how many truly different options came out of it? Three, perhaps four.

His question follows. Were there only three or four good options, or was that all the team had the time and mental bandwidth to develop and evaluate?

## What he argues

The published summary sets out the claim. For decades, limits on time and brain capacity meant teams could consider only so many options. Strategy tools such as SWOT analyses and portfolio matrices were simple because that is what planning meetings could handle.

AI, he argues, changes those limits. It can generate and evaluate a very large number of options, and build updated views of markets and competitors. It can also stress-test plans through structured debate that is not shaped by internal politics. The deck describes new tools that can improve human judgement, not replace it.

He adds a warning. Because the same tools are available to every company, lasting advantage goes to those that pair them with their own data, joined-up workflows and faster execution. In practice he points to three habits: casting a wider net before narrowing the field, replacing static models with live ones, and making AI-assisted challenge a routine part of major decisions.

## What was and was not read

Only the standfirst, the published summary and the opening paragraphs were read for this post. The rest of the article is behind HBR's paywall. No study, data set or detailed framework has been seen, so nothing here claims the article proves its case. The claim that AI can produce "a very large number" of options is the author's, and more options are not automatically better ones.

## A constraint you assumed was the world

The useful part is the shape of the question. Most advice about AI asks you to change how you work. This asks you to notice a limit you may have taken for a fact.

Think about your last planning meeting. The options on the wall were the ones your team had time to build, argue over and defend. Anything that took more than a few days to develop probably never got into the room. The offsite already happened, and the question is whether four was the answer or the budget.

It works even if your team uses no AI at all. You do not need a tool to run the check. You need the list of options you took in and a straight answer about why it was that long.

## An invented example

A regional retailer's leadership team meets each autumn to plan the year ahead. In the last meeting they discussed four ways to grow: open a new store, expand online, launch a loyalty scheme, or cut costs. All four were familiar, and each had a champion in the room.

Nobody raised partnerships with local suppliers, a subscription range, or selling through a marketplace. Those ideas needed weeks of research to cost properly, and the two days of the meeting were already full. They may have been worse ideas. Nobody knows, because they were never in the room to be argued against.

## A cheap way to test it

The steps below are our suggestion, built on his idea. They are not taken from the article.

1. **Count your options.** Before the next planning meeting, write down how many genuinely different options you expect to discuss.
2. **Ask what capped the number.** Was it the evidence, or was it time, energy and the habits of the people in the room?
3. **Widen the field first.** Ask any general AI assistant for twenty different ways to approach the decision, including some you would normally dismiss. Treat the list as raw material.
4. **Narrow it yourselves.** Your team cuts the list down using what it knows about customers, costs and people. This is where judgement sits, and it stays with you.
5. **Ask for the case against.** Give the assistant your preferred option and ask for the strongest argument against it. Read it before you decide.

Keep the people in charge of the decision. The assistant supplies breadth, and the team supplies judgement.

## Where this argument stops

There is a real risk of confusing volume with quality. Twenty options can include nineteen weak ones, and a tool that has never met your customers will not know which are naive. A longer list takes longer to sort, and the meeting still ends on a human decision.

So run it as a small experiment. Try it on one decision, keep a note of whether the wider list changed anything, and judge by that.

The question costs nothing to ask. At your next planning meeting, find out whether the number of options was the answer or the budget.
