If every company around you is raising its AI budget, you may feel pressure to do the same. A survey from the data-centre company Digital Realty suggests that many of them are spending first and measuring later.

Its headline numbers sit a long way apart. That gap is the useful part, and it is good news for anyone who wants to move carefully.

## What the survey found

Digital Realty runs an annual survey of IT decision-makers. The 2026 edition asked 2,131 of them across 19 countries. 707 were in Asia Pacific, in Australia, Hong Kong, India, Indonesia, Japan, Singapore and South Korea. The organisations had between 500 and more than 10,000 employees.

In Asia Pacific, 59% of respondents expect their AI investment to rise by more than 25% in 2026. The global figure is 55%.

Only 16% in Asia Pacific say they are realising returns on it. In Australia the figure is 33%. The same survey finds 36% of Asia Pacific organisations have AI running in real time, and 22% describe their connected infrastructure as fully integrated.

Put those side by side. More than half expect to spend a quarter more. About one in six can show what the money has produced.

## Who answered

The people asked were IT decision-makers, not the business managers who use the results. That matters. The question of whether AI pays is being answered mostly by the people who bought it, and they may not see the finance team's view.

Digital Realty also sells data-centre capacity. It has a commercial interest in a finding that points to more infrastructure. Its recommendations follow from that, so weigh the figures and set the prescription aside.

The figures here come from TNGlobal's report of Digital Realty's release, not from the release itself. The survey's fieldwork dates and method were not available when this was written. Treat the 16% as one vendor's reading until you have seen the source.

## What the gap tells you

Read the spending figure as intent. It says that budgets are being planned, not that the planned spending has worked. Intent is cheap, and it travels quickly from one company's plan to another's.

Read the return figure as an early signal. Most organisations have started, and most have yet to show a result they will put a number on. That does not mean AI fails to pay. It means the measuring is mostly still ahead.

For a department head, the comparison matters in practice. You are probably being told that everyone else has this worked out. On this evidence, most of them have not.

## Why returns are hard to show

The survey does not say why so few can show results, so this part is reasoning, not evidence. But the usual causes are easy to recognise in any workplace.

Spending is simple to count. Returns need a before and an after, and many teams never wrote down the before. A tool arrives, people use it, and nobody can say what the work looked like last year.

Time saved is the next trap. An hour saved for one person is not money unless the hour goes somewhere. If it vanishes into the day, the return is real for the person and invisible to the budget.

## What you can do with that

You do not need to match anyone's budget. You need a result you can describe to a finance director in a sentence.

A useful return is specific. "We cut the monthly report from two days to one" is a return. "We improved efficiency" is not. The first can be checked, and the second cannot.

Organisations that cannot show returns have usually skipped one step. They decided to spend before they decided how they would know it had worked.

## What to do this month

**Write down the return before you spend.** Name one task, how long it takes now, and what would count as a good result. Keep the sentence short enough to say aloud.

**Agree who will check it.** Pick someone outside the team that runs the pilot. A finance colleague is ideal, because they will ask the awkward questions you would otherwise meet later.

**Set a date to look.** Put a review in the diary for 60 or 90 days. If the numbers are not there, you have lost a small amount. If they are, you can ask for more with evidence.

**Ask your suppliers for their figures.** When a vendor says customers see strong returns, ask how many customers, measured how, and by whom.

The survey does not tell you to hold back. It tells you that going slowly, with a number in mind, is closer to the norm than it looks.
