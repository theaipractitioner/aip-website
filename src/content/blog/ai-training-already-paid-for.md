Before you budget for AI training, check whether someone has already paid for it. A regional programme in Asia-Pacific says it has trained more than half a million workers at no cost to them. Most managers have never heard of it.

That is worth a few minutes of your time, because the training budget is often the first thing that stalls an AI plan.

## What was announced

TNGlobal reported on 11 September 2026 that the ASEAN Foundation is expanding a programme called AI Ready ASEAN. It aims to give more than 1.7 million students, teachers and university staff practical AI skills by the end of 2028. The curricula are adapted locally with ASEAN education ministries.

That target is for schools and universities. The figure that matters for a department head is in the same article, and it concerns adults at work.

## The number for managers

AI Ready ASEAN sits inside a larger initiative, the AI Opportunity Fund: Asia-Pacific. It is a $35 million regional programme launched in 2024. AVPN leads it, with support from Google.org and the Asian Development Bank.

By the end of June 2026, the article says, the fund had trained more than 500,000 workers and 50,000 small businesses across Asia-Pacific.

Read that carefully. It describes a funded programme that is already running, with adults and small firms as its learners. It is not a pilot and it is not a promise.

## Who is paying, and why it matters

Google.org funds part of the initiative, and it should be named every time. It is the philanthropic arm of a company that sells AI products. A programme that teaches people to use AI serves a public purpose, and it also serves the funder.

That does not make the training poor. It does mean you should look at what is being taught before you send people. Check whether the content is general skills or tied to one supplier's tools.

The article does not say how the 500,000 and 50,000 were counted. It does not list which countries are covered. Treat the figures as the programme's own reporting, not as independent research.

## What this changes for a small team

Many managers assume AI training means a course fee, a consultant, or a day out of the office. Some of it does. Some of it is available at no cost through programmes like this one, and nobody in the department has looked.

Look at the order of work in most small firms. Someone decides the team needs training, asks for budget, waits, and the plan stalls. Meanwhile a free option sits unused because no one knew to ask.

You do not have to accept a programme as it stands. You do have to know what exists before you ask for money.

## A second signal, with a date on it

The 1.7 million target carries a message of its own. People who start work from 2028 will increasingly have been taught these skills at school or university.

That affects hiring and expectations. A graduate arriving in a few years may be more comfortable with AI tools than the manager they report to. A department that has done nothing will notice the difference.

This is a planning point, not a prediction. The target is a target, and the article gives no result for the schools programme yet.

## How to judge a free programme

Free does not mean suitable, and a quick check will tell you which. Start with the content: is it general skills such as writing instructions, checking answers and handling confidential material, or a tour of one product?

Next, look at the format. A programme that asks people to practise on their own work will change more than one that shows slides. Ask whoever tries a session whether they used something from their real week.

Finally, check what the programme asks of you. Some require registration details, a minimum team size or a commitment to report back. None of that is a problem, but it is better known before you promise the team anything.

## What to do this month

**Search for funded programmes in your own country.** Use plain terms such as "free AI skills programme" with your country and your sector. Look for government, industry body and university schemes as well as the one above.

**Check who funds each one.** Write the funder's name beside the programme. Note whether the content is general or tied to one product.

**Ask one person to try a session first.** An hour is enough. Have them report what was taught and whether it matched a real task in your work.

**Set the cost against your plan.** If a free option covers the basics, spend your own budget on the part it does not cover, such as time to practise on your own tasks.

## What the article leaves out

The article does not list the member states covered. It gives no method for counting trained workers. It does not say how long the training lasts, how much of it is practical, or whether anyone changed how they work afterwards.

Enrolment and training counts are the easiest figures for any programme to report. They tell you people attended. They do not tell you the work changed.

## The takeaway

Before you ask for a training budget, find out what is already paid for. Name the funder, check the content, and try one session before you commit the team.

Free training does not remove the harder step. Someone still has to change a real task afterwards. But it removes the first excuse, which is cost.
