Sign-ups are the easiest number to report and the least useful one to trust. A Singapore accountancy programme shows how far apart the two can sit.

The programme's own figures are clear about it. If you are counting progress on AI in your department, they suggest what to count instead.

## What the programme reports

The Institute of Singapore Chartered Accountants launched an AI Fluency programme in July 2026. It runs under Singapore's National AI Impact Programme, with the country's Infocomm Media Development Authority.

The institute reports that more than 15,000 accountants and finance professionals enrolled within two months. Its target is 60,000 over three years, so the first two months reached about a quarter of the way.

More than 250 people earned an AI Fluency digital badge. Almost 80 completed the full 30 hours of structured online learning and were recognised as AI Champions.

The curriculum covers more than 180 use cases across six roles: audit, finance, tax, internal audit, governance and board reporting. EY, Deloitte, DBS and SP Group are taking part. Singapore's Accountant-General's Department has enrolled more than 400 officers and plans to train around 4,000.

## The gap between the numbers

Put the three figures side by side. About 15,000 enrolled, more than 250 earned a badge, and almost 80 finished the full path. That is roughly one completion for every 190 sign-ups.

Do not read that as a failure rate. The programme is two months old, and many people who enrolled may still be partway through a 30-hour path. The figures describe a moment, not an outcome.

They do show something every manager should expect. Enrolment is the cheapest thing a person can do, and it is the first thing a programme reports. The later steps cost time, and they are the ones that change how work gets done.

## Why people stop

The programme's figures do not say why people stopped, so this part is reasoning and not evidence. The usual causes are easy to recognise in any workplace.

A long course competes with a full diary, and the diary wins. A first session that teaches general ideas does not feel useful on a Tuesday afternoon with a deadline. Nobody asks whether it was finished, so there is no cost to stopping.

None of these is a failing in the person. They are design problems, and a manager can change every one of them. Shorter first steps, protected time and a question about what was learned all raise the finish rate.

## Why this matters in your department

If you have started training your team on AI, you probably have a sign-up count. It will look good, because sign-ups always do.

That number can mislead you in two ways. It tells you who was willing to click a link, not who learned anything. And it makes a team feel further along than it is, which makes the next step harder to ask for.

A department head who counts sign-ups as progress will be surprised later. The surprise usually arrives when someone asks what has changed in the work.

## A claim to treat with care

The report on the programme also carries a time-saving figure. It cites a participating company, Accredify, which says its AI workflows cut time on selected finance and contract-processing tasks by an estimated 50 to 80 percent.

That figure comes from the company's own reported use case. The institute's account says it should not be read as a general productivity benchmark. Leave it out of any plan you write.

## What to count instead

Use four counts, in this order. Each one is harder to reach than the one before, and each tells you something the previous one cannot.

**Enrolled.** Who signed up. This is the number you already have, and it measures interest.

**Started.** Who opened the first session and did something with it. The drop between the first and second count tells you whether the invitation was clear.

**Finished.** Who completed the course. Ask the people who stopped why, because their answers are cheaper than a redesign.

**Using it in the work.** Who has changed one real task because of what they learned. This is the count that matters, and it is the one almost nobody reports.

The fourth count needs a conversation, not a dashboard. Ask each person to name one task they now do differently, and write down the answers.

## What to do this month

**Find your sign-up number and set it aside.** Write down how many people enrolled, then ask for the other three counts. If nobody can supply them, that is a finding.

**Ask the people who stopped.** Choose five who enrolled and did not finish. Ask what got in the way. The answer is usually time, relevance or a first session that did not feel useful.

**Shorten the first step.** A 30-hour path is a long way to ask someone to walk. Offer a first session of under an hour that ends with one task done differently.

**Report the finish, not the start.** When you update your manager on progress, lead with the count of people using it in their work. Say how many enrolled in the second sentence.

## The takeaway

A programme with 15,000 sign-ups and about 80 completions has not failed, but it has not succeeded yet either. The same is true of yours.

Count the finish, and count the use. The sign-ups will take care of themselves.
