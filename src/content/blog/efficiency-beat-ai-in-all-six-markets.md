If you run a department in this region and have not started with AI yet, you have probably been told
that everybody else has.

A survey of 3,630 people across six ASEAN markets says otherwise, and it says so with a number.

Operational efficiency is the top business priority for 35 to 43 per cent of respondents, depending
on the market. AI and technology is named a top priority by 24 to 33 per cent. Efficiency outranks
AI in every one of the six countries, and AI is a minority priority in all of them.

## What was actually measured

The study is The Great Restructuring: ASEAN Consumer and Business Pulse Survey 2026, run by
Reeracoen Group with panel supplied by Rakuten Insight. It covers Indonesia, Malaysia, the
Philippines, Singapore, Thailand and Vietnam, and was fielded in June 2026.

Stated sample, six named markets, a named fieldwork month. That combination is rarer than it should
be, and it is the reason this is worth reading rather than the size of the numbers.

On hiring, the same survey found 32 to 47 per cent of businesses reporting selective hiring, and
only 7 to 16 per cent actively expanding headcount.

## Where the interest in AI actually sits

Read the two priority figures together and something more useful appears than either on its own.

The question was not whether AI matters. It was what is at the top of the list. Efficiency won
everywhere, and AI came second or lower for two thirds to three quarters of respondents.

That is not a region rejecting the technology. It is a region where AI has to compete for attention
against margin pressure, hiring decisions and the ordinary work of running something, and where
mostly it is not winning that competition yet.

Which is a far more accurate picture of your competitors than the one you get from a conference
stage or a supplier's deck.

## The anxiety this ought to settle

The feeling that you have missed something is the most expensive emotion in this subject.

It produces two bad decisions and they look opposite. The first is buying quickly to catch up,
usually the thing that was demonstrated most recently, without a problem attached. The second is
avoiding the subject entirely because the gap feels too large to close, which compounds quietly for
a year.

Both come from the same wrong belief: that everyone else is further ahead.

Between a half and two thirds of businesses in these six markets are not expanding headcount. Three
quarters to two thirds do not have AI at the top of their list. The people you are comparing
yourself against are, mostly, also working it out.

That should not read as permission to do nothing. It should read as permission to do it properly,
because the window for a considered decision is wider than the noise suggests.

## Vietnam is the exception, and it is the sharpest number here

One figure runs against the pattern and it is the one I would sit with.

Thirty-nine per cent of Vietnamese workers considering a career change named AI and automation as a
primary motivator. Vietnam was the only market in the study where technology ranked ahead of income
growth and work-life balance as the leading reason to move.

Read that from the employer's side. In one of these six markets, the technology is already a bigger
driver of people leaving than money is.

Not because they are being replaced. Because of what it means for what their job becomes, whether
their skills still count, and whether anyone has told them what the plan is.

If your team is in Vietnam, that is a retention issue with a number attached and it is live now. If
your team is elsewhere in the region, it is the shape of a conversation worth having before it
becomes one.

## Who ran this and what they sell

Reeracoen is a recruitment company. The finding is about hiring.

That is a real interest and it belongs on the table whenever these numbers are quoted. A recruitment
firm publishing research that concludes companies are hiring selectively and thinking hard about
talent is a firm publishing research adjacent to its own business.

What keeps it usable is that the instrument is stated. The sample size is given, the six markets are
named, the fieldwork month is disclosed, and an independent panel provider supplied the respondents.
You can weigh it. Compare that with the more common alternative, which is a vendor asserting a trend
with no sample, no method and no date, and the difference is the whole reason one of them appears
here and the other does not.

## What to do with it

One exercise, and it takes fifteen minutes.

Write down the top three priorities for your department this quarter, in the order they actually
sit, not the order they would sit in a presentation.

Then find where AI is on that list. It may be first. More likely it is fourth, behind something
about cost, something about people and something about a customer.

If it is fourth, the useful question is not how to move it up. It is whether AI could help with the
three above it. That is a different question and it produces better answers, because it starts from
a problem your team already has rather than from a capability somebody demonstrated.

Efficiency is the top priority across this entire region. AI is quite good at some kinds of
efficiency. Those two facts sit closer together than the framing usually allows, and the survey has
just told you which one your colleagues actually care about.
