Most managers have watched a clear instruction dissolve on its way through the organisation. A recent Harvard Business Review article starts there, and only then reaches AI.

It is worth reading in that order. The problem exists with or without the technology, and that is the first thing to settle before you buy anything.

## The scenario

Kris Johnson Ferreira and Jordan Tong wrote the article for the September to October 2026 issue of HBR. It opens with a scenario, which the authors use as an illustration and not as a case study of a named company.

A chief executive sees that a tariff has been announced on imported goods, with a 60-day window before it applies. The logic is obvious. Buy the inventory now, before the cost rises. The CEO tells the business to pull inventory forward.

Weeks later it has not happened as intended. The order passed through procurement, logistics, finance, legal, marketing, sales and merchandising. Each round of revisions and compromises was reasonable from where that department sat. The result was, in the authors' words, "a patchwork of suboptimal actions", and the business failed to secure a large part of the goods.

## Why it went wrong

Nobody in the story did anything foolish. That is the point.

Procurement worried about supplier terms. Finance worried about cash. Legal worried about contracts, and merchandising worried about what would sell. Each concern was fair, and each one reshaped the instruction a little.

By the time the order reached the people who could act on it, it had become seven partial instructions. No one owned the whole decision. The work that decided the outcome sat between departments, and nobody's job covered the space between.

## The authors' argument about AI

Here the article turns to AI. The authors argue that most companies have pointed AI at individual tasks inside each function. A tool drafts contracts for legal, or forecasts demand for merchandising.

That, they say, is the wrong target. The work that decides performance is the work that crosses functions, and that is where little has improved. They claim a new generation of agent tools can carry a decision across departments.

A caution is needed here. The article is behind a paywall after its opening, and no companies, figures or research were visible in the part we could read. We have not seen the evidence for the central claim. Treat it as the authors' argument, not as an established result.

## What you can use without the claim

The scenario holds up whether or not the claim does. It gives a manager a test to run in their own department, and the test needs no technology.

Think of the last instruction from senior management that did not turn out as intended. Trace where it went. Who touched it, what did each person change, and who checked the result against the original aim?

If you can draw that path on a sheet of paper, you have found the place where AI might one day help. If you cannot, AI will not help yet. A tool cannot carry a decision whose route nobody can describe.

## A smaller version from your own week

You do not need a tariff to see this. A new expense rule, a change to how customers are greeted or a new approval step all travel the same way.

The manager announces it, and each team adjusts it to fit their own work. Within a month there are several versions in use, and each team believes it is following the instruction. Nobody lied, and nobody was careless.

If you have run a rollout that ended up uneven, you have already lived the scenario. The useful question is not who got it wrong. It is where the instruction first changed, and whether anyone saw it happen.

## Why this suits AI badly at first

Task-level AI is easy to start. One person tries a tool on one job, and the result is visible within an afternoon.

Cross-department work is the reverse. It needs agreement about who decides, what counts as done and who can say no. Those are management questions, and a tool does not settle them.

That is why the order matters. Decide the route and the owner first. If agents do arrive that can carry a decision across functions, they will carry whatever route you have defined, clear or muddled.

## What to do this month

**Pick one instruction that went astray.** Choose a real one from the past year, with a clear aim. Write down the aim in a sentence.

**Trace its path.** List each department or person that touched it and what they changed. Keep it to one page.

**Name one owner for the whole decision.** Not for each step, but for the outcome. If you cannot agree who, that is the finding.

**Mark where a check is missing.** Find the point where someone should have compared the result with the original aim. Put it in the diary for next time.

**Only then ask about tools.** With the route drawn, ask where a tool could save time or catch a drift. Start with one step, not the whole path.

## The takeaway

The authors may be right that AI can carry decisions across departments. Whether they are or not, the starting point is the same. An instruction that dissolves on the way down is a management problem first.

Draw the route, name the owner, and then see what a tool can add.
