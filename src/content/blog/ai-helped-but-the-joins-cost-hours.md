Most first AI rollouts end the same way. Everyone says it helped, and nothing measurable has changed. A Q&A with an executive at the software company Workday offers an explanation, and it has little to do with the AI itself.

The interview is with Jess O'Reilly, General Manager for ASEAN at Workday, published by TNGlobal on 2 September 2026. Workday sells the integrated HR and finance systems her argument points towards. Treat every number below as Workday's, not as neutral research.

## What Workday reports

Workday says 86% of ASEAN employees report that AI has improved their work experience. That is a high figure, and it matches what most managers hear when they ask how the new tool is going.

The same research reports a second finding. One in five employees lose more than seven hours a week managing data across disconnected systems. Over 80% move information between systems on a regular basis, and 82% regularly spend significant time reconciling information that does not match.

Only 30% of organisations, in Workday's figures, have embedded AI into core business operations. The rest have layered tools on top of how they already work.

The article does not state the survey's sample size or method. Keep that in mind before you quote any of it.

## Why both numbers can be true

O'Reilly's diagnosis is simple. The fragmentation between HR, finance and IT was already there. AI made an individual task fast enough that the friction around it became visible.

Picture a monthly report that took two hours to draft. With a tool, the draft takes ten minutes. The person feels faster, and says so.

The afternoon that follows is unchanged. They still check the figures against two other systems, retype a few entries, and ask a colleague which version is current. The task got quicker. The work around it did not.

That is the gap between 86% and one in five. People are reporting the part they can feel. The seven hours sit in the joins between systems, which the AI never touched.

## The risk she names

O'Reilly describes a failure mode for AI that is not connected to the data around it. The tool becomes "confidently wrong". It answers fluently from partial information, and nothing in the answer signals what is missing.

She says this matters most in HR and finance, where the margin for error is small. A wrong figure in a marketing draft costs an edit. A wrong figure in a payroll or budget check can cost a great deal more.

The point is practical. The faster a tool produces an answer, the more a manager needs to know where the answer came from.

## Her measurement advice

The most useful half of the interview is about measurement. O'Reilly recommends tracking rework, time spent reconciling information, and manual handoffs. She sets those against task-completion speed, which is the number most teams reach for first.

The logic holds without any software. If you measure how fast one task is done, you will record a success. If you measure how much of the surrounding work is still done by hand, you will find where the time went.

Task speed is also the easiest figure to collect. People can tell you how long a draft took. They find it harder to say how many times they corrected an entry, or how often they moved the same figure from one screen to another.

That difficulty is the reason to measure it. A number that is easy to collect and always flattering will not tell you when something has stopped working.

## What this means for your department

You may not run a large organisation, and you may not use Workday. The pattern still applies in most workplaces.

If your team started using AI this year, you probably have good reports of it helping. Ask what happened to the work on either side of the task. Did the checking shrink, or did it simply move?

A tool that makes one step faster while the handoffs stay manual has improved a task. It has not changed the department. Those are different results, and only the second shows up in a budget.

## What to do this month

**Pick one task your team now does with AI.** Choose one with clear steps before and after it, such as a weekly summary or a client update.

**Count the manual handoffs around it.** Write down every point where someone retypes, copies, or checks a figure against another system.

**Ask how much time goes on reconciling.** Ask the people doing the work, not their manager. A rough figure in hours per week is enough.

**Track rework for one month.** Note how often an output has to be corrected before it can be used. Compare it with what you assumed.

## What the article leaves out

The article does not give the survey's sample size, the countries covered, or the method. It does not define "disconnected systems", so a firm with three well-matched tools and a firm with ten poorly matched ones could both be counted.

Workday has a commercial interest in the finding that fragmented systems cost time, because it sells the remedy. That does not make the figures wrong. It does mean you should check the pattern against your own team before you rely on the numbers.

The friction argument and the measurement advice stand on their own logic. They are the safer part to build on.

## The takeaway

When everyone says AI helped and nothing measurable has changed, look at the joins. The time is probably in the work around the task, not in the task.

Measure rework, reconciling and handoffs for one month. Task speed will tell you the tool is fast. These three will tell you whether the department changed.
