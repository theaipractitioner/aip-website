Before you choose a first use case for AI, find out how someone else has redesigned the same kind of work. A field experiment suggests that seeing examples beats starting from a blank page.

The result comes with a caveat that changes how far it travels. The caveat is in the second section, and it matters.

## What the study reports

Harvard Business Review published an article on 1 November 2026 titled "For Real Performance Gains with AI, Start by Mapping Your Workflows". It reports research involving 515 companies. The full text sits behind HBR's paywall, so this post draws on the published summary and nothing more.

The summary says firms exposed to examples of how other organisations redesigned work around AI identified 44% more use cases. They completed 12% more tasks and were 18% more likely to acquire paying customers. They also generated nearly twice as much revenue.

## How the comparison worked

It was a field experiment. One group of firms was shown examples of how others had redesigned their work around AI. The comparison group was not shown them.

That design matters. It means the difference between the groups points to the examples themselves, and not only to the firms that chose to look. The summary does not describe how the firms were assigned or how long the study ran.

The authors also tell leaders to map their workflows from beginning to end. They add that bottlenecks tend to move as each one is solved, so the work is never finished after the first fix.

## The caveat

The 515 firms were early-stage companies. They were not established departments inside larger organisations. That limits what carries over to a manager reading this.

Paying customers and revenue make sense for a young company. A department head has neither, so the closest figures to your situation are the use cases found and the tasks completed. Treat the customer and revenue results as interesting, and not as a forecast for your team.

The summary also gives no breakdown by sector or firm size. I cannot tell you whether the effect was larger in some kinds of firm.

## Why the finding is plausible

The article summary does not explain why examples helped, so what follows is my reading and not the authors' claim. Most people asked to find uses for AI start from the tool and look for somewhere to put it. That produces short, vague lists.

An example works differently. It shows a whole process with its before and after, which makes the gaps in your own process easier to see. You recognise your own awkward handoff when you see someone else remove theirs.

That reading would also explain why the authors pair the examples with workflow mapping. You need a map of your own work to see where a borrowed idea fits.

## What counts as a good example

A good example describes work that looks like yours. It names the steps before and after the change. It says what the team stopped doing, as well as what it started.

Be wary of examples that describe only the tool. "We use AI for customer emails" tells you nothing about the redesign. "We stopped drafting replies from scratch and now check a first draft against a short list" tells you a good deal.

## What to do this month

**Find two or three peers who have changed a specific process.** Ask a supplier, an industry body or a contact at another firm. Ask about one process, not about AI in general.

**Ask each of them three questions.** What did the process look like before? Which step changed? What do you no longer do?

**Map one of your own processes from start to finish.** Mark each point where work waits for a person, a file or an approval. Those waiting points are where a borrowed idea is most likely to fit.

**Choose your first use case after that.** Write the choice as a comparison with the example you borrowed. If you cannot say what you are borrowing, keep looking.

## What the summary leaves out

The summary does not give the sample's countries or sectors, the length of the study, or how "use cases" were counted. It does not say whether the 44% came from firms that acted on the examples or only from firms that wrote more ideas down. Those are different things.

It also reports an average across 515 firms. Some firms will have gained far more, and some nothing.

## When a borrowed idea does not fit

Not every example will suit your team. A redesign that works in a firm of twelve people may fail in a department of sixty, where more people touch each step. A peer in another country may work under rules you do not share.

Treat each example as a prompt, not a recipe. Ask which part of it depends on the peer's size, rules or customers, and which part would hold anywhere. Keep the second kind and drop the first.

Test the idea on a small piece of work before you change anything large. One process, one team and a few weeks is enough to learn whether the idea survives contact with your own situation. The aim is to find out cheaply, so a poor fit costs you a few weeks and not a quarter.

## The takeaway

Do not start by asking what AI could do for your team. Ask who has already redesigned work like yours, and what they changed. In this study, the firms that looked at examples found more to do.
