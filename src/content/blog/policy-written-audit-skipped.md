A policy is a document. An audit is a habit. A new report on three Southeast Asian markets suggests the first is getting better while the second is slipping.

The report comes from a company that sells governance software, on a small sample. Read it as a prompt for a question to ask your own team, not as a verdict on the region.

## What the report says

SAS, a software company that sells AI governance tools, published its second annual Data and AI Impact Report, with research by IDC. The global study covers 2,699 decision-makers in 28 countries.

The Southeast Asia analysis was released on 15 September 2026. It combines Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand, weighted by sample. There were 122 respondents in 2026 and 120 the year before. That is a small base, and the shifts below should be read with care.

The headline is good news. The region's Trustworthiness Index rose from 57.7 to 66.5 year on year. The gap between how trustworthy firms believe their AI is and how it measures narrowed from 14.6 points to 6.6. The report says Southeast Asia is the only region to beat the global benchmark on all five dimensions.

## The numbers that went the other way

Two figures moved in the opposite direction. The share of firms running regular AI audits or impact assessments fell from 47.3% to 21.3%. The share reporting strong or high returns on their AI investment fell from 36.7% to 28.7%.

The regional Impact Index was almost flat, at 58.3 and then 58.0.

Look at the best score in the report. The responsible AI policy score is 71.2, the highest of the dimensions measured. Yet the practice of checking, which is how a policy shows up in daily work, has more than halved.

## Having a policy and running it

That gap is the useful part. Writing a policy is a project with a start and an end. A team agrees the words, someone signs them off, and the document goes on a shared drive.

Running it is different. It means someone looks, on a schedule, at what the AI is doing, and writes down what they find. It means the findings go to a person who can act.

The first kind of work finishes. The second never does, and that is why it is the one that gets dropped when other priorities arrive. A policy can improve year after year while the checking behind it quietly stops.

## What the report cannot tell you

Several limits apply. SAS sells governance software, so it has an interest in a finding that says governance needs attention. The report carries the company's name, and it should be named as the source of every figure here.

The sample is 122 firms across three markets, which is not the whole of Southeast Asia. Fieldwork dates were not stated, so the comparison is year on year and no more precise.

The report's own analysis includes a point that cuts the other way. It says the audit and accountability score rose, and TNGlobal's reading is that governance frameworks may be improving faster than recurring operational checks. Neither reading can be settled from the summary.

## What a small audit looks like

An audit does not need a software licence. At its smallest, it is one person, one hour and a sample.

Take five recent pieces of work that involved AI: a draft report, a customer reply, a summary of a meeting. Compare each with your rule. Did the person check the facts? Did they remove anything confidential? Did they say AI was used where your rule asks them to?

Write down what you find, even when everything is fine. A record that says "five checked, five fine" is worth keeping, because it shows the habit exists. It also gives you something to compare against next month.

## Why it matters in your department

You probably do not run a governance programme. You probably do have some rule about how your team uses AI, even if it is one paragraph in an email.

Ask yourself when someone last checked that the rule was being followed. If the honest answer is "when we wrote it", you have the same gap the report describes, on a smaller scale.

The question is not whether to buy software. It is who looks, how often, and what happens to what they find.

## What to do this month

**Find your rule and its date.** Locate whatever your team has about AI use. Write down when it was agreed and when anyone last checked it.

**Name the person who looks.** Choose one person and give them one hour a month. Their task is to sample five pieces of AI-assisted work and compare them with the rule.

**Write down what they find.** A short note is enough: what was checked, what was fine, what was not. Keep the notes together.

**Decide what happens next.** Say in advance who receives the findings and what they can change. A check with no route to action will be dropped in a quarter.

## The takeaway

Trust scores can rise while checks fall. When that happens, the improvement is on paper only.

Set a date for the next check before you finish reading this, and put a name beside it.
