Singapore's GovTech is building a register of every AI agent in government, recording who owns each
one and what it does. It is building that register before deploying agents across 150,000 public
officers, not after.

The order is the whole lesson. Almost every organisation will do this the other way round.

## A chatbot answers. An agent acts.

The distinction sounds like hair-splitting until you look at what it covers. A chatbot gives you an
answer and you decide what to do with it. An agent does the thing itself. It approves an invoice,
updates a customer record, moves funds between accounts.

That changes who carries the consequences. When a chatbot is wrong, someone reads the answer and
catches it, or does not. When an agent is wrong, the invoice is already paid.

Accountability for that stays exactly where it was. It sits with the manager who deployed the agent,
or authorised it, or inherited it from someone who left. In most organisations that manager cannot
see what the agent actually did, which is an uncomfortable combination: full responsibility, no
visibility.

## What Singapore is actually doing

Three things are worth taking seriously here, and all three are checkable rather than promised.

IMDA, Singapore's Infocomm Media Development Authority, has published a Model AI Governance
Framework for Agentic AI. Keeping humans meaningfully accountable is one of its four pillars, which
is a stronger position than most governance documents take. It does not say a human should be
consulted. It says a named human remains answerable.

GovTech is building the ownership register described above, ahead of the 150,000-officer rollout.
Not a policy about registers. An actual list.

Singtel has restructured its corporate organisation to manage blended human and AI teams. That is a
telecoms company deciding its org chart needs to account for something that is not a person and is
not a tool either.

None of that requires you to care about Singapore. It matters because it converts an abstract worry
into something with a shape: somebody built the list, and they built it first.

## The failure has a name

The risk being guarded against is AI sprawl. Agents created without central oversight, drifting past
their intended purpose, reaching data they were never meant to touch.

What makes sprawl worth naming is that nobody decides it. It accumulates. One team automates a
handover. Someone in finance sets up a reconciliation that runs overnight. A supplier's tool starts
doing something on your behalf that nobody quite authorised, because authorising it was a checkbox
in an onboarding flow.

Each of those is reasonable on its own. The problem is only visible in aggregate, and only if
somebody is keeping the aggregate.

## A register is not a policy

There is a version of this that goes wrong, and it is worth naming because it is the more likely
outcome. An organisation reads about agent governance, agrees it matters, and writes a policy.

A policy says what should happen. A register says what is happening. Those are different documents
and only one of them tells you anything you did not already know. The policy will say agents must be
approved and owned. The register will tell you about the three nobody approved, which is the
information you were actually missing.

Start with the register. If it turns out to be short and tidy, you have spent an hour and confirmed
something useful. If it turns out to be longer than you expected, you have found the reason to write
the policy, and you will write a better one for having seen the list first.

## The test most departments will fail

Here is the version you can run this week, and it takes an hour.

Name every AI agent operating in your area. Not every AI tool: every agent, meaning every piece of
software that takes an action rather than producing a suggestion. Then name the person accountable
for each one.

Most managers cannot complete that list. More to the point, most have never been asked to, which is
why the gap has not surfaced. It is not a competence failure. Nobody has requested the inventory, so
nobody has kept it.

If your answer is that you do not have any agents yet, that is the best possible time to write the
register. A list of nothing takes five minutes and establishes the habit before there is anything to
reconstruct. The version of this exercise that takes three weeks is the one done in eighteen months,
backwards, from expense reports and vague recollections.

## What the register needs to hold

Four columns will do.

What the agent is, in a sentence a non-specialist can read. What it is permitted to act on, stated
as a boundary rather than a purpose. Who owns it by name, not by team. And what happens if it is
wrong, including who finds out and how quickly.

That last column is the one people skip, and it is the one that turns a list into a control. An
agent nobody monitors is not governed by having been written down.

## Who is telling you this, and what they sell

The article that carried the Singapore material is a contributed column by Karl Crowther, Area Vice
President for Middle East and Africa and South Asia at UiPath. UiPath sells agent orchestration
software.

His recommendation is that organisations build an orchestration layer before scaling. That may well
be right, and it is also his employer's product, so treat it as a position rather than as advice.

The IMDA framework, the GovTech register, the 150,000 officers and the Singtel restructure are
different in kind. They are facts about what named organisations have done, and you can check every
one of them without going anywhere near a vendor. Build on those. His two best lines survive the
disclosure intact: accountability cannot be coded, and you cannot manage what you cannot see.

## The part that costs nothing

Registers are unglamorous and nobody gets promoted for keeping one. The argument for writing yours
now is narrower than governance and more practical.

At some point in the next two years, someone is going to ask what your department has running and
who is answerable for it. That question arrives either as a planning exercise or as an incident. In
the first case the list is an afternoon. In the second it is an archaeology project conducted under
pressure, in front of people who are not in a generous mood.

Singapore is doing the afternoon version, for 150,000 people, before switching anything on.
