The first useful AI project in your department may not need anything new. A Singapore software company and a Dubai contractor say they got results by pointing AI at cameras that were already on the site.

The numbers are the companies' own, and they come with gaps. Even so, the way the customer describes the result is worth copying.

## What happened

viAct is a Singapore-based company that sells AI video analytics. Alliad is a contractor headquartered in Dubai. They ran the software over Alliad's existing site cameras on a remote healthcare construction project in Côte d'Ivoire.

No new cameras were installed. The software watched the footage the cameras already produced.

Over three months, the companies report that non-compliance with personal protective equipment rules fell by 41.7%. Violations of danger zones fell by 46.3%. Personal protective equipment means the hard hats, vests and similar gear that site rules require.

## Whose numbers these are

The figures belong to viAct and its customer. TNGlobal, which reported the story on 28 September 2026, notes that viAct did not disclose several things. It gave no size for the deployment, no count of cameras, no baseline number of violations and no independent audit.

The missing baseline matters most. A fall of 41.7% from twelve breaches is five fewer. A fall of 41.7% from twelve hundred is five hundred fewer. Without the starting number, the percentage cannot tell you which one happened.

It is also one site, over three months, chosen and reported by a company that sells the product. Read the percentages as a report of direction. They are not a measurement you can plan around.

## What three months can and cannot show

A short window flatters a new tool. People behave differently when they know something has changed, and a fresh system gets attention that fades. A fall in the first quarter says little about the third.

Other things may also have moved during those months. A new site manager, a stricter induction or a change in the work being done can all reduce breaches. The report does not say whether any of these happened, and neither could a reader.

None of this means the result is false. It means the honest reading is "this looks promising at one site", and you should hold your own pilot to the same standard. Run it long enough to see the novelty wear off, and note what else changes while it runs.

## The claim worth believing

The customer's account is more modest than the vendor's numbers. Mounir Ziani, who heads QHSE for the engineering and construction division at Alliad, describes the system as an extra layer of real-time monitoring. In his account, it helped the team spot possible compliance problems sooner.

That is a small claim, and small claims are easier to trust. He does not say the software replaced the safety team or ran the site. He says the team heard about problems earlier than before.

This is the pattern to look for in any case study. A manager who describes an earlier warning has told you what changed in their week. A vendor who promises transformation has told you what they want you to buy.

## The question about watching people

Cameras on a worksite also watch workers. That deserves a straight answer before anyone copies the idea.

The reports of this project describe checks on safety gear and danger zones. They do not say what else was monitored, who sees the footage or how workers were told. If you are thinking of something similar, decide those three things in writing first. Tell the people on camera before you start, not after.

## What a department head can take from it

You are probably not running a construction site. The lesson still travels, because most departments sit on records they do not use well.

Think about what your team already has that records something. Cameras are one example. So are shared inboxes, booking systems, quality checklists, delivery logs and weekly spreadsheets. Each one holds a stream of events that somebody checks late, unevenly or not at all.

AI is often good at the checking and poor at the deciding. Pointing it at a stream you already own gives you a way to start that needs no new equipment and no new process. The people still make the call.

## What to do this month

**List what you already own that records something.** Aim for five items. Include the dull ones. The best candidates are usually the logs nobody has time to read.

**Pick one check that happens late or unevenly.** Choose something where an earlier warning would change what your team does. A late delivery flagged on the day is more useful than one found at month end.

**Count the baseline before you start.** Write down how many problems you find now, over a fixed period. This is the number the vendor in this story did not share, and it is the one you will need to judge any result.

**Write the small claim.** Finish the sentence "we would hear about this sooner by", in one line. If you cannot, the project is too big for a first attempt.

**Tell the people affected first.** Say what is being checked, who sees the results and what will not be done with them. Ten minutes at a team meeting prevents months of suspicion.

## The takeaway

The percentages in this story are unproven. The shape of the project is sound: use what you have, aim at one check, expect an earlier warning, and measure from a known starting point.

If a supplier offers you a bigger claim than that for a first project, ask for the baseline.

*Source: TNGlobal, 28 September 2026, reporting viAct and Alliad. The figures are the companies' own and were not independently audited. Details of the deployment were not disclosed.*
