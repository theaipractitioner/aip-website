If you manage a team, you may be the person who decides whether AI works at your company. Not the board, not the vendor, and not the training portal. A recent Harvard Business Review article makes that argument, and it describes your position.

Most advice on AI adoption is addressed to executives. This article is addressed, in effect, to the layer below them.

## What the article argues

Gleb Tsipursky wrote the piece, published by HBR on 1 September 2026. Its central claim is that generative AI initiatives fail most often at the middle-manager level. He writes that the failure point is "the layer of managers who must turn executive ambition into everyday behavior".

He adds that middle managers decide what AI becomes in their part of the organisation. It can become a real workflow, a side experiment, a compliance burden, or something the team quietly avoids.

Here I need to be plain about what I have read. The article is behind a paywall, and I have seen only the headline, the standfirst and the opening lines. The author goes on to set out profiles of different kinds of manager. I have not read them, so I will not describe them.

## Why the argument is plausible

You do not need the paywalled detail to see why the claim has force. Think about how an AI decision travels through a company.

A senior leader announces that the company will adopt AI. A vendor is chosen and a training course is bought. None of that changes what anyone does on Monday morning.

What changes Monday is a manager saying yes to a new way of working, or not. That manager decides whether the team has time to learn, whether a mistake will be forgiven, and whether the old process may be dropped. The decision lands in their diary, not in the strategy document.

## Four questions to ask yourself

The article is reported to look at how different managers respond. I cannot tell you its categories, so here is a frame of my own. It is not a summary of the article. These four questions cover most of what makes a manager say yes or no.

**What is the risk to me?** If a tool produces a wrong answer in your team's name, who answers for it? A manager who carries that risk alone will hold back, and sensibly so.

**What is the evidence?** Have you seen the tool do a task from your own work, with your own material? A vendor demonstration is not evidence about your department.

**What are the incentives?** If your team is measured on volume and accuracy this quarter, time spent learning is a cost. Check what you are actually rewarded for.

**What support exists?** Is there someone you can ask, an hour in the week to practise, and a clear rule about what is allowed? A manager without support has to invent all three alone.

## What each answer tells you

If your honest answer to the first question is "I would be blamed", you do not have an AI problem. You have an accountability gap, and no amount of training fixes it.

If the second answer is "I have only seen a presentation", the next step is small. Run the tool on one real task and compare the result with what you would have produced.

If the third is "nothing rewards this", say so to your own manager. It is a reasonable thing to raise, and it is better raised than silently acted on.

If the fourth is "no support", ask for the cheapest version: one named person to ask, and a short written rule.

## What this means for your team

You may think your role is to pass instructions down. On AI, your role is closer to a gate. Your team will do what you make easy, safe and worthwhile.

That is not a comfortable position, and it is not meant to be blame. It means a small change from you has more effect than a large announcement from above.

It also means doing nothing is a decision. A team whose manager neither encourages nor forbids AI will drift into a mix of private use and avoidance.

## What to do this month

**Answer the four questions in writing.** Do it for yourself first, with one sentence each. Do not show anyone yet.

**Pick the weakest answer.** Most managers find one that is clearly worse than the rest. Work on that one before anything else.

**Run one tool on one real task.** Choose something your team does weekly. Compare the output with your normal result, and keep notes.

**Tell your team what is allowed.** A short written rule, even three lines, removes the fear that using AI will be held against them.

## What I cannot tell you

I cannot tell you what the article recommends in full. I cannot tell you which evidence the author relies on, or how many managers he draws on. The paywalled sections may qualify the claim in ways I cannot see.

The argument is the author's, and the four questions are mine. If you can read the full piece, do, and compare it with your own experience.

## The takeaway

The decision about AI in your team sits with you, whether or not you asked for it. Make the four answers visible to yourself before your team makes the choice for you.

Start with the weakest answer and one real task. That is enough for a month.
