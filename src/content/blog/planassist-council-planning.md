A UK planning tool shows a pattern any department head can copy. Give AI the high-volume simple cases, and keep a named person with the final say.

The figures behind it come from the company that built the tool, so they need careful handling. The design does not.

## What DeepMind reports

Google DeepMind published a post on 30 October 2026, written by Owen Larter and David Thacker, about a tool called PlanAssist. DeepMind built it with the UK government, Google Cloud and Faculty. It helps council planning officers assess routine householder applications, such as loft conversions and extensions.

Hold one fact in mind throughout. DeepMind developed the tool and is the only source for the figures below. Read each one as "DeepMind reports", not as an independent finding.

## What the tool does

PlanAssist checks that an application's documents and drawings are complete. It checks heritage constraints, local and national policy, and earlier objections. It then drafts a report with citations, which the officer edits before any decision.

None of this is exotic. It is the checking work that sits in front of a judgement, and that describes most of the routine paperwork in any department.

## The figures

The pilot ran from June in three councils: Barnet, Camden and Dorset. DeepMind reports that average officer time on householder assessment tasks fell by 58%, and its standfirst says "up to 58%". It also reports that more than 70% of the generated reports were approved by officers with no edits.

One more figure explains why the choice of case matters. DeepMind says routine householder applications make up up to 70% of a planning officer's workload. The tool was aimed at the largest and simplest slice of the work.

## What the figures do not say

The post does not give baseline hours, so a 58% fall cannot be turned into minutes saved. It does not say how many applications were assessed or how officer time was measured. It does not say how many reports were edited or rejected, and it cites no independent evaluation.

Three councils is a small early sample, and all three are in England. A report approved without edits is also not the same as a correct decision, because an officer under time pressure may approve a plausible draft. DeepMind's own statement of the control matters here: planning officers keep the final say on every application.

## The rollout so far

DeepMind describes a 12-month beta on live applications. Twelve more councils will join in three cohorts, which brings the total to 15. The government plans to offer the tool to all councils in England in 2027, subject to successful testing.

That is a plan, not a decision. Treat the 2027 date as an intention that depends on the beta going well.

## The pattern to copy

Strip out the planning detail and three moves remain. They work in any team that handles a stream of similar requests.

**Pick the high-volume simple case.** PlanAssist did not start with contested or unusual applications. It started with the type that arrives most often and follows the clearest rules.

**Keep a named person with the final say.** The officer reviews and approves every assessment before any decision. The tool prepares the checking, and a qualified person owns the outcome.

**Measure against a baseline.** DeepMind reports time and the share of reports approved with no edits. Those are the two numbers worth tracking in your own pilot, and both need a starting point recorded before the tool arrives.

## What to do this month

**List the case types your team handles every week.** Write the rough volume of each. Most teams find that one or two types account for most of the work.

**Choose the simplest of the high-volume types.** Pick the one with the clearest rules and the lowest cost of a mistake. Leave the unusual cases with people for now.

**Name the person who signs off.** Write down who reviews the AI's draft and what they are checking for. If you cannot name them, you are not ready to start.

**Record two numbers before you begin.** Time one person takes on a typical case today, and how often finished work is sent back for correction. Without those, you cannot tell later whether anything improved.

## What the post leaves out

DeepMind's post is a developer describing its own pilot. It does not describe what officers thought of the drafts, what the edited reports looked like, or what happened to the rejected ones. It does not say what the councils pay.

A planning authority is also an unusual setting. Its rules are published and its case types are narrow. A department that handles varied work, with rules that live in people's heads, may find the pattern harder to apply.

## A warning about "approved with no edits"

The 70% figure is the easiest one to misread. It sounds like accuracy, and DeepMind's post does not claim that. It counts reports an officer approved without changing them.

Approval is a human act, and humans get tired. If an officer has forty applications waiting, a clear and confident draft is easy to wave through. The risk is not that the tool is careless. The risk is that a good draft lowers the reviewer's guard.

If you copy this pattern, build a way to catch that. Have a second person check a small sample of approved drafts each month. Track how often the sampled drafts turn out to need a change the first reviewer missed. That number tells you whether the review is working.

## The takeaway

Look for the case type that arrives most often and follows the clearest rules. Give the checking to AI, and keep a named person responsible for the decision. Record your baseline first, so the result means something.
