The first AI tool a department builds should probably face its own staff, not its customers. A Singapore insurer offers a small example. The figures come from the insurer and from the company that sold it the tool, so each one needs careful handling.

## What was reported

TNGlobal reported on 18 September 2026 that Singlife, a Singapore insurer, runs two Salesforce AI agents in its customer-service operation. TNGlobal states that the figures were supplied by Salesforce and Singlife and have not been independently audited. Salesforce sells the agents, so both companies have an interest in the result.

Please read every number below as theirs.

## The two agents

The first is a General Insurance Email Agent. It reads customer emails and routes them. It answers some enquiries directly. Singlife says it handles more than 20% of email enquiries.

The second is called Buddy. It is internal. It sits beside customer-service executives and helps them find answers while they work.

Singlife credits Buddy with a 30% cut in average handling time. It also credits Buddy with cutting onboarding for new staff from four weeks to two.

## The figure that matters

Handling time gets the attention, because it is easy to count. The onboarding figure is the more interesting one.

Four weeks to two is a claim about how fast a new person becomes useful. It is not a claim about replacing anyone. A new starter with an assistant that finds the answer can work on live enquiries sooner, and gets fewer things wrong while learning.

That is a different kind of gain from the usual story. It concerns the time between hiring someone and trusting them with the work.

## Why the pairing matters

Look at the two agents side by side. One faces customers and one faces staff. The result the insurer highlights belongs to the staff-facing one.

TNGlobal reports that Buddy is next moving to a customer-facing role across digital channels. So the order was staff-support agent first, customer-facing later.

That order is the lesson. An assistant used by your own people fails in front of colleagues, who can spot a wrong answer and correct it. An assistant speaking directly to customers fails in front of the people you most want to keep.

## What you can borrow

You do not need a Salesforce agent, and you may not have a customer-service team. The pattern applies to any department with a steady flow of new people and a body of knowledge they have to absorb.

Think about what a new starter in your team asks in their first month. Where is the template, what is our rule on this, who approves that. Those questions have answers that already exist, written down somewhere.

An internal helper that finds those answers is a lower-risk first move than anything a client will see. It tests whether your own information is in good order, which is the real precondition for everything else.

## How to read a figure from the seller and the buyer

When a vendor and its customer report the same result together, it is tempting to count that as two sources. It is closer to one. The customer is describing a tool it chose and paid for, and the vendor is describing a sale it made.

Neither is lying, and the figures may be perfectly good. The point is that nobody with no stake in the outcome has checked the method. A fair reading holds the number loosely and asks what you would need to see to trust it.

For a claim like "four weeks to two", the first thing to ask is how the start and end were defined. Did onboarding end when the new person finished a course, or when they handled a normal case alone? A change in the definition can move the figure as much as a change in the tool.

## What to do this month

**Collect the questions.** Ask your newest colleague, and the one before them, to list what they had to ask in their first month. Aim for twenty.

**Check where the answers live.** For each question, note whether the answer is written down, who owns it, and when it was last updated.

**Time a new starter.** Write down how long it takes before someone can handle a normal task alone. You need a baseline before a claim like "four weeks to two" means anything.

**Start internal.** If you try an AI helper, give it to staff first, with a named person who checks its answers.

## What the article leaves out

The article gives no staff numbers, no rollout timeline and no description of the pilot design. It does not say how handling time or onboarding was measured, or which kinds of enquiry were included.

It does not say how many new starters went through the process, so the four-to-two figure could come from a small group. I have not invented any of those details, and you should not assume them.

The customer-facing step is a plan, not a result. Nothing in the report shows how it went.

## The takeaway

If you are choosing where to start with AI, look at the internal helper before the customer-facing tool. It fails more gently and teaches you more about your own information.

Measure how long a new person takes to become useful, before and after. That figure is harder to inflate than a percentage of handling time.
