Here is a test you can run on any AI tool in your department, whether or not you run a contact centre. Imagine switching it off tomorrow, and ask what would break.

The test comes from an interview with a contact-centre executive. The numbers around it need care, but the test does not.

## Where the test comes from

TNGlobal published an interview on 17 September 2026 with Michael Cowell, Managing Director of TDCX Hong Kong. TDCX is a Singapore-headquartered company that runs customer-experience operations for other businesses. It sells AI-enabled contact-centre delivery, so Cowell is describing his own product, not an independent view.

Hold that in mind for everything below. His claims concern TDCX's own operation, and nobody has audited them in the article.

## The old number

The figure you can rely on is the old one. Cowell says a traditional contact centre may review around 2 to 5 percent of its interactions for quality. The rest goes unchecked.

That describes how quality assurance has always worked. A supervisor listens to a sample of calls, or reads a sample of chats, and scores them. Nobody has the hours to do more.

The new claim is TDCX's own. Cowell says that with AI-assisted review the company can now look at effectively every contact. That may be true of TDCX. The article gives no evidence of accuracy, so read it as a claim and not as a result.

## The test

The most useful thing in the interview is a question Cowell puts to any operation. If you switched the AI off tomorrow, what would break?

He describes two possible answers. In an operation that has added tools, things get slower. People go back to doing the same work by hand, a little less efficiently.

In an operation that is genuinely built around AI, the process stops. It was designed on the assumption that the AI is there, so removing it removes a step the process cannot do without.

## Why the test is useful

Most departments that have "adopted AI" fall into the first group. The tool sits beside the work. It helps, and people would miss it, but the work goes on without it.

That is not a failure. It is a description. It tells you the tool has made an existing process faster and has not yet changed the process.

The second group has made a bigger bet. The process now depends on the tool, which creates a new risk: if the tool fails, changes, or is withdrawn, the work stops. Someone should know that, and have a plan.

Either answer is informative. The mistake is not knowing which one you have.

## Another line worth keeping

Cowell also says the most important changes are the ones customers never see. That runs against how AI is usually sold, which is with a visible assistant or a new chat window.

The point carries over to any team. The biggest gains may be in checking, routing and preparation behind the scenes, not in a feature a client can point to.

## Two operating details

The interview gives two figures about delivery. Cowell says it takes 60 to 90 days to get a new pilot live. That is his estimate for his own operation, and a useful reminder that a pilot is a project with a timeline.

It also reports that TDCX opened a campus in Foshan in September with about 100 positions, around 90 minutes from Hong Kong Central. That sits oddly with a story of AI simply removing people. The interview gives no figure for roles changed, so do not draw a conclusion either way.

## How to run the test properly

The test is a thought experiment, so it is easy to answer too quickly. A careful answer comes from the people who do the work, not from the person who bought the tool.

Ask each person what they would do first on the morning the tool vanished. If they can describe the manual route clearly and say how long it takes, you have an operation where work would slow. If they hesitate, or say nobody remembers how it used to be done, you have found a dependency.

Watch for the middle case as well. Some steps would continue by hand while others would simply stop, such as a check that only exists because the tool makes it cheap. Those stopped steps are the ones to write down, because they show where the process has quietly changed.

## What to do this month

**List the AI tools your team uses.** One line for each, including ones people use on their own.

**Ask the switch-off question for each.** What would happen tomorrow if it were gone? Write the honest answer, not the hopeful one.

**Sort them into two groups.** Those where work would slow, and those where work would stop. Look hard at the second group.

**Make a plan for anything that would stop.** Name who would notice, who would decide, and what the manual fallback is.

## What the interview leaves out

The article does not say how accurately the AI reviews calls, or how a person checks its judgements. It does not say what TDCX's clients pay or how many contacts are covered. Cowell is the only voice.

A contact centre is also an unusual setting, with a high volume of similar interactions. A small team doing varied work may find the test gives a less clear answer.

## The takeaway

Ask what would break if you switched the AI off tomorrow. If the answer is "we would be slower", you have added a tool. If it is "we would stop", you have built a dependency.

Neither is wrong. Know which one you have.
