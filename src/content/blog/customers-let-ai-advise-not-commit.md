Customers are comfortable with AI that suggests. They are far less comfortable with AI that decides and acts for them.

A survey of 6,000 consumers puts numbers on that gap. It is also a useful test for any service you run, because the line between advising and committing is easy to find once you look for it.

## What the survey found

Alipay+, the wallet gateway of Ant International, published the study with S&P Global in early September 2026. It surveyed 6,000 consumers across nine markets in Asia, Europe and the United States about cross-border travel and spending.

The headline pattern runs in three steps.

- **81.3%** already use AI tools to explore destinations and experiences.
- **73.2%** said they would consider AI for hotel or flight bookings over the next twelve months.
- **26%** expressed interest in using AI for autonomous payment decisions.

Comfort is high while AI helps people look and plan, and it drops sharply as AI moves toward spending their money. The report also found 43% citing privacy concerns, 43% preferring to plan travel themselves, and 32% preferring human advice to AI assistance.

## A word on who paid for it

Alipay+ is part of Ant International and sells payment technology. It has a commercial interest in a finding that customers want trusted, well-controlled systems around AI payments. S&P Global is named as the research partner.

The press coverage read for this post does not list the nine markets, give the full questionnaire or report a margin of error. So this post makes no claim about any one country, and it treats the survey as a company-commissioned study, not independent academic research.

One more caution. The survey is about consumers and travel. It does not measure how staff at work feel about AI making decisions, and the 26% reflects how the question was framed. The pattern is the useful part, not the exact figures.

## A second source points the same way

Checkout.com, a payments company, published its own Agentic Commerce 2026 report in June 2026. According to Fintech News Singapore's account, it surveyed 400 heads of payments at consumer-facing businesses and more than 12,000 consumers globally in the first quarter of 2026. It found many consumers are not yet ready to delegate spending.

It also asked what controls people would insist on before letting an AI shopping agent buy for them. The most common answers were:

- spending caps (30%)
- the ability to revoke permission instantly (29%)
- an easy cancellation or reversal window (28%)
- approval above a set threshold (27%)
- being shown options before the purchase (26%)

Checkout.com also has a commercial interest. Two vendor-commissioned surveys are not proof, but they were run by different companies on different groups of people, and they agree on the direction. That agreement is stronger than either figure alone.

## What the gradient means for your service

Strip out the travel and the pattern is simple. A customer will accept AI that helps them find, compare and understand. Acceptance falls when AI takes an action they cannot easily undo, such as paying, applying, cancelling or agreeing.

Take any customer journey in your business and mark each step as one of two kinds.

- **Advising:** the tool suggests, summarises or drafts, and the customer decides.
- **Committing:** the tool does something that costs money, changes a record or binds someone to a term.

Every committing step is where you should expect hesitation, and where a visible human check earns its place. The reason is not that the AI is likely to be wrong. The reason is that a large share of customers say they will not accept it without a say.

## An invented example

A small insurance broker uses AI to help customers choose a policy. The tool compares three quotes, explains the differences in plain words and highlights which one suits the customer's circumstances. Customers like it, and few complain.

Then the broker adds a button that lets the tool renew the chosen policy automatically each year and update the payment card. Complaints rise, and several customers switch the feature off. The tool did not become less accurate. It moved from advising to committing, and the customers noticed. A short renewal reminder, with a one-click approval and a clear way to cancel, would have kept the benefit and answered most of the worry.

## What to do with this

1. **Map one journey.** Pick a customer process that now involves AI and list its steps in order.
2. **Mark each step advising or committing.** Be strict. If the customer cannot easily reverse it, it commits.
3. **Add a visible check to each committing step.** That might be a confirmation screen, an approval above a set amount, or a named person who reviews it.
4. **Offer controls before you are asked.** The Checkout.com list is a ready checklist: a cap, an instant way to revoke, a reversal window and a threshold for approval.
5. **Watch where customers drop out.** If people abandon the journey at the committing step, you have found the trust gap in your own data.

## Where this argument stops

The finding comes from consumer surveys paid for by payments companies. It tells you how people say they feel, not how they behave when offered a good deal, and attitudes may shift quickly. Your customers may differ from the survey's, and a business customer buying for a company may weigh the risk differently.

What it does give you is a sorting rule. Before you automate the last step of a process, find out whether it advises or commits, and who agreed to that.
